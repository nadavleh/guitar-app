# Progression Songs — Playlist Source

Famous songs for every progression in the app's ear-training **progression library**
(the same lists shown when you tap a progression row). It is laid out so you can walk
top-to-bottom and drop each `Title — Artist` into a Spotify playlist.

- **Major / Minor (diatonic)** lists are ubiquitous, unambiguous hits.
- **Advanced** and **Circle of fifths** lists are *characteristic* examples — a song
  that prominently features the signature harmonic move, not a promise of a
  note-for-note match.
- A song may appear under more than one progression when it genuinely uses both.

Generated for app version **v1.19.0**. Keep in sync with
`theory/.../ProgressionSongs.kt` and `chorect-web/src/theory/progressionSongs.ts`.

## Advanced (non-diatonic)

*Characteristic examples — the signature harmonic move, not always note-for-note.*

### Mixolydian Rocker — I – bVII – IV
Borrows bVII from the parallel Mixolydian mode for a driving, anthemic classic-rock sound.
- Sweet Home Alabama — Lynyrd Skynyrd
- Takin' Care of Business — Bachman-Turner Overdrive
- Werewolves of London — Warren Zevon
- Gloria — Them
- Cocaine — Eric Clapton

### Bright Lift — I – II – IV – I
The major II is a borrowed/secondary-dominant chord (V of V) that gives a sudden, hopeful lift.
- Eight Days a Week — The Beatles
- Don't Think Twice, It's All Right — Bob Dylan
- 9 to 5 — Dolly Parton
- Tequila — The Champs

### Romantic Climax — I – III – IV – iv
A bright major III then a borrowed minor iv — a dramatic rise melting into melancholy.
- Creep — Radiohead
- Get Free — Lana Del Rey
- In My Life — The Beatles
- No Surprises — Radiohead

### Epic Backstep — I – bVII – bVI – bVII
Borrowed bVII and bVI from the parallel minor give a cinematic, heroic backstep.
- Rolling in the Deep — Adele
- Stairway to Heaven — Led Zeppelin
- China Girl — David Bowie
- All Along the Watchtower — Jimi Hendrix
- Citizen Erased — Muse

### Andalusian Cadence — i – bVII – bVI – V
The flamenco descending tetrachord; the major V (harmonic minor) adds dark, Spanish tension.
- Hit the Road Jack — Ray Charles
- Sultans of Swing — Dire Straits
- Runaway — Del Shannon
- Stray Cat Strut — Stray Cats
- California Dreamin' — The Mamas & the Papas
- Happy Together — The Turtles

### Dark Roots — i – iv – v
Uses the natural-minor v (minor, not the usual major V) for a raw, modal folk/blues feel.
- Back to Black — Amy Winehouse
- Heartbreak Hotel — Elvis Presley
- Ain't No Sunshine — Bill Withers
- Bury a Friend — Billie Eilish

### Neo-Soul Minor — i – v – bVI – bVII
Moody natural-minor motion through a minor v, popular in modern R&B and lo-fi.
- Redbone — Childish Gambino
- The Bird — Anderson .Paak
- Get You — Daniel Caesar
- Location — Khalid

### Ragtime Circle — I – VI7 – II7 – V7
A chain of secondary dominants around the circle of fifths — the bouncing staple of ragtime and stride.
- Sweet Georgia Brown — Brother Bones & His Shadows
- Five Foot Two, Eyes of Blue — The Ink Spots
- Alice's Restaurant — Arlo Guthrie
- Hey! Baby — Bruce Channel
- Salty Dog Blues — Rev. Gary Davis

### Classic Ragtime Turnaround — I – I7 – IV – iv
I becomes a dominant I7 to tonicise IV, then a borrowed minor iv adds a nostalgic, bluesy turn.
- Ain't She Sweet — Gene Austin
- Bill Bailey, Won't You Please Come Home — Bobby Darin
- When You're Smiling — Louis Armstrong
- Baby Face — Al Jolson

### Chromatic Passing Chord — I – #I°7 – ii7 – V7
A #i diminished passing chord connects I to ii7 with a smooth chromatic walking bass.
- Ain't Misbehavin' — Fats Waller
- I Got Rhythm — George Gershwin
- Have You Met Miss Jones? — Frank Sinatra

### Traditional Rag Ending — I – III7 – IV – #IV°7 – I/V – V7 – I
A syncopated Scott-Joplin ending: a secondary-dominant III7, a #IV°7 passing chord, then a I–V7–I cadence.
- Maple Leaf Rag — Scott Joplin
- The Entertainer — Scott Joplin
- The Easy Winners — Scott Joplin
- Solace — Scott Joplin
- Twelfth Street Rag — Euday L. Bowman

### Melancholic Jazz-Rag — I – III7 – iv – ii7b5 – V7
A secondary-dominant III7 leads to a borrowed minor iv and a half-diminished ii — bittersweet and vintage.
- Georgia on My Mind — Ray Charles
- My Melancholy Baby — Gene Austin
- After You've Gone — Bessie Smith
- Somebody Stole My Gal — Bing Crosby

### Broadway Lift — I – III7 – IV – ii7 – V7
The secondary-dominant III7 brightens a major-key ii–V cadence — a classic show-tune lift.
- On the Sunny Side of the Street — Louis Armstrong
- Georgia on My Mind — Ray Charles
- Someday My Prince Will Come — Snow White (Disney)
- Just a Closer Walk with Thee — Traditional

### Minor-Key Swing — i – III7 – iv – ii7 – V7
Starts dark, then a striking secondary-dominant III7 lifts before the ii–V cadence.
- Bei Mir Bist Du Schön — The Andrews Sisters
- Bésame Mucho — Dean Martin
- Minor Swing — Django Reinhardt

### Extended Pop Ballad — I – III7 – vi – IV – ii7 – V7
A secondary-dominant III7 tonicises vi, prolonging tension before the ii–V resolution.
- Come Rain or Come Shine — Ray Charles
- There Will Never Be Another You — Chet Baker
- Eternal Flame — The Bangles
- The Shoop Shoop Song (It's in His Kiss) — Cher
- One Call Away — Charlie Puth

### Tritone Substitution — ii7 – bII7 – Imaj7
The dominant V7 is replaced by bII7 a tritone away — a smooth chromatic slide into the tonic.
- The Girl from Ipanema — Antônio Carlos Jobim
- Body and Soul — Coleman Hawkins
- Night and Day — Cole Porter
- Autumn Leaves — Nat King Cole

### Minor Line Cliché — i – i(maj7) – i7 – i6
A stationary minor chord with one inner voice descending chromatically (root–7–b7–6).
- My Funny Valentine — Chet Baker
- James Bond Theme — Monty Norman
- Michelle — The Beatles
- Stairway to Heaven — Led Zeppelin
- This Masquerade — George Benson

### Romantic Plaintive — I – Imaj7 – I7 – IV
A major line cliché: the top voice melts down (root–maj7–b7), pulling toward IV.
- Something — The Beatles
- Can't Take My Eyes Off You — Frankie Valli
- I Just Called to Say I Love You — Stevie Wonder
- Kiss Me — Sixpence None the Richer

### Church Cadence — I – IV – I – bVII – IV
A gospel plagal feel with a bluesy bVII descent back to IV.
- Hey Jude — The Beatles
- Sweet Home Alabama — Lynyrd Skynyrd
- Takin' Care of Business — Bachman-Turner Overdrive
- Fortunate Son — Creedence Clearwater Revival

### Gospel Walk-Up — I – I/III – IV – #IV°7 – V
A bassline climbing the scale through a #IV°7 diminished chord — a driving gospel walk-up.
- On the Sunny Side of the Street — Louis Armstrong
- Embraceable You — Ella Fitzgerald
- The Song Is You — Frank Sinatra

### Mario Cadence — bVI – bVII – I
Borrowed bVI and bVII resolve up to a triumphant major I — the classic heroic/video-game cadence.
- Super Mario Bros. (Level Complete) — Koji Kondo
- The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time — Koji Kondo
- Final Fantasy (Victory Fanfare) — Nobuo Uematsu
- Star Wars (Main Title) — John Williams

### Royal Road — IV – V – iii – vi
The backbone of modern J-pop/anime: it loops without ever landing on the home chord.
- Never Gonna Give You Up — Rick Astley
- Titanium — David Guetta & Sia
- Leave the Door Open — Silk Sonic
- Fortnight — Taylor Swift & Post Malone
- Heavy Rotation — AKB48
- Yoru ni Kakeru — YOASOBI

### Bird Blues Turnaround — Imaj7 – #IV°7 – iii7 – VI7 – ii7 – V7
Charlie Parker's rapid descending turnaround, stacking a passing #IV°7 and a secondary-dominant VI7.
- Blues for Alice — Charlie Parker
- Au Privave — Charlie Parker
- Chi Chi — Charlie Parker
- Bloomdido — Charlie Parker

### Montgomery Turnaround — Imaj7 – bIII7 – bVI7 – bII7
A highly chromatic Wes-Montgomery turnaround that slides back to the tonic in tritone steps.
- Lady Bird — Tadd Dameron
- Half Nelson — Miles Davis
- Israel — Miles Davis
